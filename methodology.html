Methodology and scoring rubrics

How Cobalt's Buyer Evidence Coach (v0.6) rates K-12 edtech vendors. The tool rates what vendors say about their products against the research available, so that buyers can see where claims are well founded and what to ask next. It rates claims, not intent, and it is a proof of concept still being tested.

Four measures, never combined

Each vendor is rated on four separate measures:

  • Strength of evidence: how good the research on the product is.
  • Marketing credibility: how closely the vendor's claims match that research.
  • Outcome studies: how willing the vendor has been to have its impact tested and the results published.
  • Ongoing monitoring: what the vendor and its customers can track about implementation and outcomes in everyday use.

The last two appear together under "Evidence commitment": one is about proving that a product works through studies, the other about knowing whether it is working in a given district.

They are not added up into a score because they often diverge, and the divergence is the information. A vendor can have strong research and overstated marketing, or modest research described accurately.

Sources and search

Claims come from findable marketing assets: website pages (including older pages still publicly reachable), research and impact pages, press releases, case studies, blog posts, product pages, FAQs and information packets. Sales decks and RFP responses are included when a buyer supplies them; the reviews in this proof of concept used public materials only.

Evidence is gathered beyond the vendor's own research page:

  • every study the vendor cites, read at the level of results tables rather than executive summaries or abstracts;
  • clearinghouses and registries: Evidence for ESSA, What Works Clearinghouse, CASEL Program Guide, EdReports, Blueprints, the Education Endowment Foundation and others where relevant;
  • grant databases (IES, NSF, major foundations) and trial registries, to find studies that were planned or completed but never published;
  • scholarly searches, including dissertations and studies outside the US.

Each study is checked to confirm it tested this product (not a similar program or an earlier version) and measured the outcome the claim describes. Ratings that rest on an abstract or on a figure with no stated source are marked provisional in the tile's explanation.

What counts as a claim

A claim is a statement in the vendor's voice about the product's effectiveness or outcomes, or about the quality, independence or status of its evidence. A customer testimonial counts when the vendor features it and it states an outcome.

Two claims are distinct when each would need a different finding to support it, defined by outcome, population (grade, subgroup, product) and size or timeframe. "Higher English scores" and "higher math scores" from the same study are two claims; so are "grades K–1" and "grades 1–3." Each distinct claim gets one tile, however many pages repeat it.

Headline claims (outlined tiles) are the lead claim on the homepage, research page, product page, press release or impact report, plus any claim that the product is "proven," "independent," "peer-reviewed" or meets an ESSA tier.

Claim ratings

RatingMeaning
AccurateMatches the findings, with limitations acknowledged where relevant.
Somewhat overstatedSupported in direction but stretched in scope, population, size or certainty.
Substantially overstatedGoes well beyond what the evidence shows.
UnsupportedNo evidence offered for this product, or the cited source did not measure the claimed outcome.
ContradictedThe evidence points the other way, or the claim misdescribes a source, including its design or independence.

Every tile's explanation states what the evidence supports before what it does not.

Common checks

These recurring patterns account for most ratings below Accurate:

  • Summary drift: claims are rated against results tables, because overstatement often starts in a study's own executive summary.
  • Source does not measure the claim: a bullying claim that cites studies of grades and engagement is Unsupported.
  • Design misdescription: calling a quasi-experiment "randomized" is at least Substantially overstated.
  • Relative framing: "17% fewer days suspended" may be a very small absolute change.
  • Pooling: combining grades, sites or implementation levels to claim breadth that only one subgroup shows.
  • Construct substitution: usage presented as learning, enrollment as attendance, classroom quality as school climate, or a curriculum quality rating as evidence of impact.
  • Borrowed evidence: research on the problem a product targets, or on similar programs, presented as the product's own results (Unsupported).
  • Product relabeling: evidence on an earlier version, a more intensive model or another country's program presented under the current product's name.
  • Causal language from non-causal designs: "proves" or "produced" based on before-and-after or correlational data.
  • Older pages: pages still publicly reachable count. Corrections and removals are credited.

Marketing credibility indicators

  • In line or modest stretch: Accurate plus Somewhat overstated claims, over all evaluable claims (shown as "x of y claims"). Rows in the table are ordered by this share.
  • Headlines: the same count for headline claims only.
  • Go further than the evidence: claims rated Substantially overstated, Unsupported or Contradicted.
  • Limitations in marketing: whether the marketing itself mentions limitations or null findings. Candor confined to technical reports is noted but not credited.

Both the share and the count matter. A vendor with many specific claims has more that can be found overstated; a vendor with few claims can swing several points on one rating. Shares based on fewer than about 10 evaluable claims are fragile.

Strength of evidence

Rated separately by grade band, product component or outcome when results differ.

LevelTypical basis
StrongRandomized trials or multiple well-designed quasi-experiments, independent of the vendor, in relevant populations and outcomes.
ModerateAt least one well-designed quasi-experiment with baseline equivalence, a well-designed randomized trial run by the developer, or correlational evidence with strong controls. Vendor-commissioned studies are acceptable.
LimitedBefore-and-after or descriptive data without a comparison group, vendor analyses of customer data, or comparisons with one school per condition.
MinimalA logic model, testimonials, usage figures, or research on the program category only.

Ratings also weigh design details that affect confidence: how many schools were in each condition, whether nesting in classrooms and schools was modeled, who chose what was observed, missing data, the number of tests run against the number that were significant, survivor effects in multi-year customer data, and which product version was studied.

Independence

Studies are classified by who ran them, from most to least independent:

  • Independent: run by an organization with no ties to the vendor, or commissioned by the vendor and passing the transparency test below.
  • Commissioned, not public: a commissioned study by an outside organization that fails the transparency test.
  • Developer-led academic study: peer-reviewed research by the program's university-based developers.
  • Vendor-analyzed: analyses by the vendor's own staff.
  • Developer-affiliated author: a current or former employee, founder or equity holder.

Transparency test. A commissioned study counts as independent when an outside organization ran the analysis and either the full report is public, including null results, or the study was pre-registered, or the contract guaranteed publication.

Claims of independence are rated by the gap between the claimed and the actual type: commissioned but passing the test, Accurate; failing it, Somewhat overstated; developer-led, Substantially overstated; developer-affiliated author, Contradicted. An ESSA certifier's review is not independent conduct, and calling it "peer review" is Contradicted.

Evidence commitment: outcome studies

Rated on what the vendor has done, not its research capacity. Staff headcount and the number of studies do not count by themselves.

LevelDefinition
NoneNo outcome studies: usage data, testimonials or category research only.
In-houseAt least one before-and-after study or stronger, without a comparison group, run and reported by vendor staff with methods and limitations stated. Norm-referenced growth counts here.
SingleOne rigorous outcome study with a comparison group, not necessarily independent. Announced studies don't count until results appear.
RepeatedAt least two comparison-group outcome studies, credibly reported (methods and results public, null results included), not necessarily independent.
SustainedMultiple outcome studies over several years, at least two of them independent, more underway or announced, and results published regardless of outcome.

An independent trial that has closed without public results caps the rating below Sustained until results appear. It is reported as pending, without inferring the outcome. Outcomes-based contracts are noted where found.

Evidence commitment: ongoing monitoring

The second "evidence commitment" column describes the vendor's impact measurement practice as Cobalt conceptualizes it: whether the vendor routinely shows how well a product is implemented and tracks outcomes within customer districts. The outcome studies column captures what a vendor has proved through studies; ongoing monitoring captures what it and its customers can know in ordinary use. It is a description, not a rating, and covers two things:

  • Implementation support and monitoring: how the vendor measures use, fidelity or practice (for example usage dashboards, lesson-completion tracking, fidelity checklists, coaching logs).
  • Routine outcome monitoring: evidence that the vendor can relate implementation to outcomes in a customer's own data, such as embedded or norm-referenced assessments reported back to schools, usage–outcome analyses in customer districts, or outcomes-based contracts.

Public materials show only part of this. A vendor's actual practice can be stronger than what its website describes, and these descriptions should be confirmed with the vendor.

Badges

Badges are listed as issued, naming the organization, and are never re-rated. They fall into two groups:

  • Impact ratings: ESSA tiers from third-party reviewers and other outcome-based ratings (for example Evidence for ESSA, Blueprints, the Education Endowment Foundation). Certificates the vendor paid for are marked "(commissioned)."
  • Other reviews: certifications of program design, content quality or state approval (for example CASEL SELect, EdReports, Digital Promise). These do not measure impact.

What a vendor says a badge shows is rated as a claim. Displaying an EdReports score on an impact page is construct substitution; displaying an outdated ESSA rating is Contradicted.

Quality control and limits

  • AI-assisted ratings. Ratings were produced with an AI-assisted rubric and reviewed by Cobalt. A sample was re-rated blind by a second AI rater; the two agreed on most claims, and disagreements moved results by about one claim.
  • A snapshot. Ratings reflect public materials reviewed in November 2026. Vendors update their pages, and studies are published.
  • Not a purchasing recommendation. Ratings describe the fit between claims and evidence. They do not rate product quality or a vendor's intent, and they do not tell a district what to buy.
  • Conflicts disclosed. The analyst has professional ties to several vendors in this set; this is one reason names are withheld.

Planned before any public use: two independent raters on every claim with adjudication, a documented evidence record behind each tile, a response window for vendors to correct errors, version history for every rating, and recusal for conflicts.
